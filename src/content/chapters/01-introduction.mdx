# Chapter 1 - Computer Brain

## Introduction - The Most Powerful Thing in the Universe

Computer is the most powerful thing in the universe. At least as of our today’s knowledge.

A computer can calculate the trajectory of a spacecraft, simulate the formation of galaxies, design new molecules, and control machines far more powerful than itself. Its power comes not from what it physically is, but from what it can do with information.

We know computers that come in many forms.

Some are made of silicon, with billions of transistors performing calculations. Others are mechanical, using gears and levers. Quantum computers exploit the behavior of matter at its smallest scales. As long as the systems can process information, respond to signals, and regulate behavior - it is a computer.

And one of the most important computers we know is sitting inside your skull (assuming the reader is a human).

**Your brain**

Unlike a conventional digital computer, the brain is made of living cells. It’s execution of software instructions is quite different from what is running inside silicon-based CPUs. Yet it performs many of the same fundamental functions: receiving input, processing information, storing memories, recognizing patterns, and producing output.

To understand how we experience reality, how we think, and why we behave as we do, we must first understand the machinery that makes it all possible.

**That is the purpose of this book:** to explore the brain's underlying mechanisms, understand how they interact across different levels, and build a coherent picture of the system as a whole.

But why do a reader needs it? I believe, that professionalism and growth of a computer is measured by 3 major criteria:

1. Details - how deep you can look into the subject, and how many minute pieces of information can you notice. Chess master see much more information on the chess board than a novice who sees a box full of squares for the first time.
2. Patterns - in the constant flow of information how many patterns do you recognize and can use to your advantage. Every pattern consists of many of it's own details. Chess master play defense pattern on the right side and offence by the center for example. And observe how opponent's moves change the dynamics of those patterns.
3. Dimensions - from how many angles you can look at the subject. Every new angle gives you another dimension. Every dimension consists of its own patterns. And every pattern consists of details. Chess master can consider the day of the match, his/her or opponent's emotional state, location, and many more factors that can influence the game outcome.  Dimensions is the level that is not fully comprehensible by our brain due to its infinite complexity. This is the job for the next computer in the evolutionary chain that we are building - AI.

Your brain is your tool. The same way as all other parts of your body and the whole body itself is the tool. And the more proficient you are at utilizing your toolset the better you are at living a life. Professionalism opens possibilities that are not available to novices. So as we understand better the underlying mechanics of the brain, better life choices we can make, more opportunity doors can open and achieve higher personal and professional goals.
I think this is a good enough justification for writing a book.

## Main Claims

The catch gives us a useful starting point for understanding the brain: a living system that transforms signals into expectations and action. Six claims describe that system.

**The brain is a biological computer.** It receives information, changes its internal state, and produces responses. Past experience alters how it performs those transformations. The computer analogy helps us follow this work. It becomes misleading if we imagine a central processor executing fixed instructions, with memory in one compartment and a user sitting somewhere above it. In the brain, learning changes the machinery itself.

**Pattern is its primary unit of work.** A ball approaching your face means something different from a ball rolling away. The difference lies in relationships among shape, motion, distance, and your body. We will call such organized relationships patterns. This is the book’s vocabulary for connecting sensation, learning, and action; it is not the name of a universally agreed biological unit.

**The brain processes information from the external world with a delay.** By the time information can guide a response, its source has moved on. The delay is usually unobtrusive, but the moving ball makes its consequences visible.

**The brain continuously runs an internal simulation.** During ordinary waking life, it maintains a changing account of what is happening and what can happen next. “Simulation” names this working model. It need not resemble a detailed movie, and it does not imply the same conscious activity during sleep, anesthesia, and wakefulness.

**The brain is a prediction machine.** It prepares for events before they are fully known, then adjusts when new evidence arrives. Prediction is central to the account developed here. How far one predictive theory can explain the whole brain remains an open scientific question.

**For the brain, fantasy and reality exist within the same internal simulation.** Imagine catching the ball again. Some of the machinery that represents a seen movement can also help represent an imagined one. The overlap is partial: imagining and perceiving are distinguishable activities, and the brain can often identify which is taking place. Shared machinery makes imagination possible; it does not make fantasy and external events neurologically identical. [Source placeholder: imagery–perception overlap and source-monitoring research.]

These claims concern a brain inside a body. Its information has consequences: a missed catch, a spoken answer, a step away from danger. That connection between representation and consequence will keep the computer metaphor grounded.

## Biological Computer

### Neural System

The movement of your hand begins in living tissue. Neurons receive influences from other cells, and changes in the voltage across their membranes affect whether they send signals onward. An action potential travels along a neuron. At many connections, its arrival releases chemical messengers that alter another cell’s activity. Some connections transmit electrical changes more directly. [Source placeholder: neuronal signaling, chemical synapses, and electrical synapses.]

No single neuron needs to know that you are catching a ball. The task depends on activity distributed across interacting networks: information about movement, body position, and possible actions affects what happens next. Different regions specialize, but they cooperate through overlapping pathways. A brain divided into rigid software modules would miss much of this interaction.

Practice changes the relationships among cells and networks. Connections can strengthen or weaken; the conditions under which activity spreads can change. This capacity, called plasticity, helps explain why your hundredth catch differs from your first. [Source placeholder: synaptic plasticity and motor learning.]

A child initially reaches too soon, closes the hand too late, or misjudges the distance. With experience, the movement becomes better coordinated. Learning has altered the system that will attempt the next catch.

### Sensory Inputs

The ball never enters your brain. Light reflected from its surface reaches your eyes, where receptor cells respond. Pressure on the skin and movement within muscles provide other kinds of information when your hand meets it.

Different receptors respond to different physical conditions. Light changes the activity of photoreceptors. Mechanical forces support touch, pressure, vibration, and hearing. Molecules interacting with receptors contribute to smell and taste. Temperature-sensitive mechanisms respond to warmth and cold. Other sensory systems register balance, body position, and conditions inside the body. [Source placeholder: sensory transduction across these systems.]

These processes convert physical effects into neural signals. The brain receives the resulting activity, already shaped by the properties of the receptors and their pathways. It receives neither an untouched external world nor a ready-made description of one.

Calling the signals “encoded” means that features of stimulation correspond to features of activity: which cells respond, when they respond, and how their responses change. No tiny reader needs to translate a message inside the skull. The signals influence other cells, and those interactions carry the processing forward.

### Motor Outputs

When your fingers close, neural activity has changed something outside the nervous system. Muscles pull, joints move, and the ball stops. The basic relationship is a loop:

**External event → sensory signal → neural processing → motor output → changed external conditions**

The changed conditions become new input. Pressure increases against your palm. The ball’s visible motion slows. Signals from your arm register the load. The catch continues through these adjustments rather than ending with a single command.

The same loop extends into conversation. Muscles shape a word; someone hears it and responds. A raised eyebrow or a turned shoulder can change what another person does. Spinal and peripheral processes contribute throughout; the brain does not individually supervise every contraction.

Attention helps guide the loop by selecting information and directing movements of the eyes or head. Attention itself is not a motor output, but it can determine which action makes fresh evidence available. Looking behind an object changes what you can know about it. The body is both a means of acting and a means of finding out.

### Processing Capacity

While watching the ball, you probably do not count the leaves behind your friend. Their colors may reach your eyes, yet most never become details you could report afterward.

The brain has finite resources. It must filter, prioritize, and compress. Compression preserves useful relationships while dropping detail: the ball is approaching from the left; the exact arrangement of marks on its surface may be irrelevant. Available information greatly exceeds what can receive equal attention or enter awareness.

In experiments by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, people watching a video were asked to count basketball passes. Many missed an unexpected figure crossing the scene, including a person in a gorilla costume. Concentration on the assigned task left something conspicuous unnoticed. [Source: Simons and Chabris, 1999, “Gorillas in our midst,” https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059.]

Practice reduces the need to supervise familiar actions deliberately. You can catch without narrating each joint movement. Automatic processing still uses resources and can still fail; it simply does not require every adjustment to become a conscious decision. Awareness is a selective view of the work underway.

### Processing Delay

A signal has a journey to make. Light must reach the eye; receptors must respond; activity must travel and interact with other activity. Muscles also take time to develop movement.

These delays vary with the pathway and the task. Turning toward a sudden change need not wait for a detailed identification of its source. There is no single refresh rate at which the whole brain finishes one complete picture and begins the next.

Prediction helps overcome the practical consequences. As you reach, your movement reflects an expected path, not just the last position for which information became available. Integration across time helps produce an apparently continuous experience. Neither process makes transmission instantaneous. [Source placeholder: sensorimotor delay compensation and temporal integration.]

The distinction becomes obvious when the ball takes an unexpected bounce. Your hand may briefly continue toward the place it was prepared to meet it. The mistake reveals the forecast that a smooth catch would have concealed.

## Pattern

### Definition

A ball moving toward you and a ball moving away can produce similar colors and outlines. What changes is the relationship among successive signals. That relationship determines whether you prepare to catch or watch it leave.

A **pattern** is an organized relationship among signals, neural activity, stored associations, or actions. Organization includes timing and context, not just shape. A familiar voice saying your name from across a crowded room has a different significance from the same voice in a recording.

Patterns are distributed, context-dependent configurations. Some describe activity unfolding now; others describe lasting changes that make particular activity more likely later. A stored pattern need not be a physical object placed in a neural drawer.

The catch lets us follow five roles a pattern can play: arriving information, learned preparation, current activity, expectation, and action. These roles overlap within a working nervous system.

### Input Pattern

The ball grows larger in your visual field. Its position changes. At the same time, signals from your shoulder, elbow, and wrist provide information about where your hand is.

This configuration is an input pattern: sensory activity arriving from the environment and the body. The relationship between the two matters more than either alone. A perfectly estimated ball position would be of little use if you had no information about the hand meant to intercept it.

The input is incomplete. Part of the ball may be hidden, and some movement may be difficult to judge. Its signals provide evidence from which a useful estimate can emerge; they do not deliver a finished trajectory.

### Stored Pattern

Earlier catches have left your nervous system better prepared for this one. Repetition has shaped connections and response tendencies linking what an approaching ball looks like with movements that have worked.

A stored pattern is this relatively persistent organization, formed through experience. “Stored” does not require ongoing activity or a complete record of a previous event. A changed network can respond differently when suitable input arrives again. [Source placeholder: persistent neural changes supporting learning.]

What you learn can include variation. Soft balls give under pressure; heavier ones require more support. Experience supplies a range of useful relationships rather than one motion to replay regardless of circumstances.

### Active Pattern

As the ball approaches, some learned relationships begin influencing what happens now. Your hand opens. Its expected meeting point becomes important. A conversation nearby may recede from awareness.

An active pattern is a configuration currently shaping processing, feeling, awareness, or behavior. Some of its influence is visible to you; much is not. You can prepare a coordinated reach without knowing which muscular adjustments support it.

Context determines which relationships become influential. During a game, you reach for the ball. When both hands hold a fragile glass bowl, the same approaching object may prompt a turn of the body. Recognizing the object does not settle the action.

### Predicted Pattern

Before contact, your nervous system can prepare for a particular sequence: the ball approaches, pressure appears against the palm, and the fingers meet resistance as they close.

That expected configuration is a predicted pattern. It can concern movement, touch, sound, or bodily load. You need not consciously picture it. An expectation can guide preparation without appearing as an image or a thought.

The forecast also has limits. A clear, slow throw offers better evidence than a ball suddenly emerging from behind someone. Preparation can accommodate several possible outcomes, with different degrees of confidence. Prediction does not require pretending that the future is certain.

### Output Pattern

Your arm moves into position and your fingers close. An organized pattern of neural activity has helped produce coordinated action.

An output pattern can also organize a word, a facial expression, a physiological adjustment, or a decision that changes readiness to act. These outputs differ: a decision is not itself a muscle contraction, and internal bodily regulation extends beyond skeletal movement. The common feature is that processing changes what the organism does or prepares to do.

The successful catch produces a new input pattern: pressure, weight, and a stationary ball. Output has changed the conditions the system encounters next. The five roles describe a circulation of information through action, not a one-way assembly line.

## Pattern Operations

The ball is only one object, but dealing with it requires several kinds of work. It must become recognizable, relevant, and connected to an action. The following names distinguish that work. They form a conceptual model rather than an established taxonomy of neural functions; one neural process can serve several roles at once.

### Recognition

You recognize a ball even when its color is unfamiliar or half of it is hidden behind your friend’s fingers. Enough relationships remain for the object to become identifiable.

Recognition links current input to something previously encountered, whether an individual object or a category. It tolerates differences. The same ball produces different sensory configurations as it moves through sunlight and shadow, yet it can retain its identity.

This stability matters for action. If every changed view had to be treated as an entirely new object, useful experience could scarcely carry forward. Recognition makes previous learning available to the present encounter.

### Matching

Recognition gives an answer: a ball. Matching concerns how well the evidence fits a stored or predicted relationship. Does the changing outline fit an approaching sphere? Does the movement fit a gentle throw?

A match can be partial. Several interpretations may fit some features, with later input favoring one. Matching is therefore useful after recognition too: you can identify the ball correctly and still misjudge its speed.

The comparison need not resemble a search through labeled photographs. Networks respond differently to different configurations. Those differences can allow one interpretation or action to gain influence as the incoming evidence changes.

### Activation

Seeing the throw can prepare your hand before you consciously decide to catch. The recognized event has activated related patterns of response.

Activation means that one configuration increases the influence of another. Connections learned through experience can bring memories, feelings, and actions into play. A remembered injury may contribute caution; familiar play may support an easy reach.

The relationships do not all activate equally. The same object encountered during a relaxed game or a sudden disturbance can lead toward different preparations. What comes to mind—and what the body begins doing—depends on the current setting as well as the object.

### Combination

The ball’s motion is only part of the situation. Your friend’s stance, the distance between you, and the position of your hand contribute additional constraints. Together they can support an interpretation: a throw you can comfortably catch.

Combination assembles several patterns into a larger relationship. Each source helps resolve uncertainty left by the others. A hand moving forward becomes easier to understand when it belongs to someone holding a ball and looking toward you.

The combined interpretation can be wrong, but it gives action a richer basis than isolated signals would. You respond to an event taking place, not simply to a collection of colors and movements.

### Competition

Now suppose the throw comes sharply toward your face. Reaching, ducking, and stepping aside offer different solutions. They cannot all fully direct the body at once.

Competition describes how incompatible interpretations or responses gain and lose influence. Sensory evidence matters, but so do goals and past consequences. A practiced catcher may reach; someone protecting an injured hand may move away.

Often the competition settles without conscious debate. When it does not, you may hesitate or begin one movement and switch to another. The awkwardness is visible, while the alternatives that produced it may remain difficult to describe.

### Inhibition

You begin reaching. Your friend shouts, “Wet paint!” The hand stops.

Inhibition restrains activity or limits its influence. A prepared response can lose its hold as another constraint becomes relevant. Coordinated behavior depends on this selectivity: producing a useful action also involves preventing incompatible ones from disrupting it.

Neural inhibition is a specific biological process; stopping an action is a broader function involving networks. The two should not be equated as though every act of restraint came from one inhibitory cell. [Source placeholder: inhibitory signaling and response-control networks.]

In the catch, inhibition helps make the eventual movement orderly. It is part of organization, not simply the absence of action.

### Updating

The ball lands more heavily than expected. On the next throw, you brace your hand differently.

Updating changes the relationships that guide later processing. A discrepancy can revise the immediate movement, alter the current interpretation, or contribute to lasting learning. Those changes need not occur together.

One unusual throw may deserve only a local correction. Repeated surprises may indicate that your expectations about this ball are poorly suited to it. The system must distinguish a temporary disturbance from a relationship worth learning.

That distinction makes error useful. The question is not merely whether the prediction failed, but what should change because it failed: the reach, the estimate of the ball, or the expectations carried into future catches.

## Internal Simulation

### Delayed Sensory Information

Imagine a camera showing a road through a delayed feed. The picture can be accurate about what happened and still be inadequate for steering through what is happening now.

The nervous system faces a related problem, although it is no passive camera. Its evidence always has a history. The ball has moved since the light now guiding your response left its surface.

The internal simulation is the working account that lets the organism act despite that history. It incorporates new evidence while preserving useful continuity and anticipating change. Experience presents a moving ball in a usable space, rather than a series of dated sensory reports.

The account remains vulnerable to correction. A missed catch is one way the external world exposes the difference between what the system represented and what occurred.

### Reconstruction of the External World

Hold the ball still. Four different things participate in this apparently simple experience.

**The external world** contains the ball, your hand, and their physical interaction. **Physical signals reaching sensory receptors** include reflected light and mechanical pressure. **Neural representations** are the patterns through which those effects influence the nervous system. **Conscious experience** is the ball as it appears and feels to you: round, close, resting against your palm.

The distinctions matter because the relationship is not a direct copy. Receptors capture selected effects, neural systems organize them, and you encounter a coherent object. The construction draws on incomplete signals, learned patterns, context, and expectations.

In Edward Adelson’s checker-shadow illusion, two patches with the same gray value appear different. Their surroundings suggest different illumination, leading the visual system toward different surface interpretations. Knowing that the patches match may leave their apparent difference intact. [Source: Edward H. Adelson, MIT, https://persci.mit.edu/gallery/checkershadow.]

The illusion exposes a process that usually helps objects remain stable across lighting changes. Construction supports contact with reality, even while making systematic errors possible. How neural activity gives rise to subjective experience remains a further question; naming a simulation does not answer it.

### Memory

You know how the ball will probably feel before it touches you. Earlier encounters participate in the current one without your having to recall a particular afternoon or throw.

Memory supplies learned relationships that help organize the present. Deliberate remembering uses retained information too, but reconstructs an event under the conditions of recall. It is not an untouched recording waiting to play.

In Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer’s accident-film experiments, the wording of questions influenced speed estimates and later reports of broken glass. The results concern memory and reports; they do not establish that the original visual experience changed afterward. [Source: Loftus and Palmer, 1974, “Reconstruction of Automobile Destruction.”]

The practical consequence is a porous boundary between retained detail and current interpretation. Familiarity can help identify what is happening, yet it can also make an expected detail feel as though it was observed. Past experience gives the present structure without guaranteeing accuracy.

### Imagination

Set the ball down and imagine throwing it. You may picture its arc, anticipate the movement of your arm, or hear the imagined impact against a wall. The physical ball remains still.

Imagination activates representations without matching sensory input from a current event. Research finds partial overlap between mechanisms supporting imagery and perception, alongside differences in activity and direction of influence. People also vary in how vividly, or visually, they imagine. [Source placeholder: imagery–perception comparisons and individual differences in imagery.]

This overlap allows an absent event to influence preparation and feeling. An imagined missed catch can make you cautious before the next throw. It does not follow that the imagined event is mistaken for a real one.

The nervous system can use contextual information, sensory detail, and the circumstances in which a representation arose to judge its source. Those judgments sometimes fail. [Source placeholder: source-monitoring research.]

Fantasy and perception thus share some resources within the working simulation while remaining distinguishable. Imagination expands what the system can represent beyond what its receptors currently detect.

### Predicted Future

A future can be represented without being deliberately imagined. Your reaching hand demonstrates this: preparation anticipates contact even when no picture of the catch appears in awareness.

Similar anticipation occurs across daily life. A briefly hidden moving object can remain expected along its path. The beginning of a sentence narrows the words likely to follow. In conversation, a pause and a change of expression help prepare your turn. Predictive contributions differ across these tasks; they need not depend on one identical neural routine. [Source placeholder: prediction in perception, language, and social timing.]

Anticipation gives an organism time to respond before danger arrives. It also creates a risk of unnecessary action. A falsely expected collision can interrupt movement just as a missed collision can leave the body unprepared.

The value of a prediction lies partly in its revisability. Acting ahead of complete evidence is useful only when the system remains sensitive to what the next evidence reveals.

## Prediction Cycle

The catch and the false alarm expose the same practical problem: how to prepare before knowing everything, then change when more becomes known.

A five-part cycle captures the solution. It is a functional account, not a claim that the brain executes five separate instructions in order. The processes can overlap, recur, and operate at different levels.

To follow them, keep the approaching ball in view. Then place another scene beside it: you wake at night and see what looks like someone standing near the bedroom door.

### Generate Prediction

Your friend releases the ball. Its initial movement, the distance, and previous catches support an expected path. Your hand begins preparing for contact at a likely place and time. The prediction concerns both the event and your relationship to it.

In the bedroom, the evidence is sparse: an upright shape, a rounded top, something like a shoulder. A noise has woken you. The interpretation “someone is there” gives these fragments a common cause and makes further human movement seem possible.

Neither preparation requires a consciously stated forecast. You can reach before articulating the ball’s trajectory and tense before identifying the shape. Expectations become visible in what the system prepares to do.

### Receive Input

The ball curves slightly. Further visual information arrives while signals from your moving arm indicate the progress of the reach. The movement now observed differs from the movement initially anticipated.

At the bedroom door, headlights from a passing car sweep across the wall. A sleeve becomes visible. Above it, the rounded shape has a strap. The scene offers details unavailable in the darkness.

New input supplies evidence, not an explanation printed on the objects. Its clarity matters. The curve may be easy to detect; a faint change in shadow may remain ambiguous. What the system can revise depends on what its sensory equipment can distinguish at that moment.

### Compare Input with Prediction

The ball is no longer approaching the place your hand was prepared to occupy. The expected relationship between its path and your movement has weakened. A correction becomes necessary before contact.

At the door, the visible strap and sleeve fit a bag hanging above a coat. They fit a person less well. An alternative interpretation gains support from the same scene that produced the first one.

In predictive accounts, such comparisons can arise through interactions among neural populations, without a conscious observer inspecting two pictures. The relevant question is whether incoming activity supports the relationships guiding current processing. Comparison keeps an initially useful interpretation open to revision.

### Calculate Prediction Error

Prediction error is the discrepancy between the expected pattern and the incoming one. In the catch, it concerns position and timing. In the bedroom, it concerns what kind of thing occupies the doorway.

“Calculate” refers to processing a difference, not necessarily to arithmetic or an error number accessible to awareness. Different predictive models describe different errors and mechanisms. A conscious feeling of surprise is not a direct measurement of them. [Source placeholder: predictive processing and sensorimotor error models.]

The discrepancy also needs weight. A brief flicker may be unreliable; clear illumination deserves more influence. If weak evidence overturned every expectation, experience would be unstable. If expectations overruled every discrepancy, the system would stop learning from the world. Effective revision depends on the relationship between evidence and confidence.

### Update Patterns

Your hand shifts, meets the curved path, and closes around the ball. The current action has changed. If the next throws follow the same curve, you may begin preparing differently from the outset. Correction within one catch can become learning across many.

In the bedroom, the intruder disappears. The coat and bag have not moved. What changes is the interpretation organizing them into a person. Your body may remain tense briefly after the shape becomes recognizable; visual understanding and bodily preparation need not settle at the same instant.

Both scenes have completed the cycle. An expectation guided preparation; new input challenged it; the discrepancy changed the interpretation or action. One adjustment delivered a ball into your hand. The other removed a person who had never stood beside the door.

The successful catch makes this work easy to overlook. The coat makes it difficult to deny. In each case, the experienced situation arrives already organized, with likely causes, possible consequences, and actions becoming available. The outside world constrains that organization through what happens next.

**The brain does not wait for reality to arrive and then display it. It continuously constructs a working simulation, tests that simulation against incoming signals, and updates it through prediction error.**

Your hand met a future before it arrived. Your eyes found a person where a coat was hanging. Both events occurred in an experienced world assembled by the same living system.

Inside that world, someone seems to be looking, deciding, and acting.

What—or who—is that someone?
